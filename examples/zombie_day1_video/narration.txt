You wake to a dog stopping mid-bark.
The little clock on the wall is dark.
Your phone shows nothing when you press it.
The fridge has stopped humming in the kitchen.
The curtain shifts when you touch it.
The street below looks exactly as it did yesterday.
A bus stands at the stop with its door open.
A traffic light changes colour for nobody.
Nobody walks and nothing moves on the whole street.
Then a woman steps out of the bus shelter.
She walks to the middle of the road and stops.
Her head hangs forward as if she is listening to the ground.
She does not look up.
Her shoulders rise and fall too slowly.
A man stands with his forehead against a shop window.
He stays there, perfectly still, in yesterday's coat.
You notice that neither of them looks at the other.
They only turn when something makes a sound.
A bottle falls from a bin and both heads snap toward it.
They follow noise, not faces.
Three knocks come at your front door.
They are small knocks, low down on the wood.
You know that knock.
It belongs to the boy from the flat below.
He knocks there when his ball goes over the wall.
You cross the room and put your eye to the peephole.
He stands barefoot on the cold tile.
One hand rests flat against your door.
In the other hand he holds a dog lead.
The lead is empty.
There is no dog at the end of it.
His face is turned away from the glass.
He knocks again, three small knocks.
You put your hand on the lock.
Your fingers stop there.
There is a dark stain down the front of his shirt.
And he has not blinked once.
You watch him through a hole the size of a coin.
He is the only person you have spoken to all week.
He knocks again and you do not answer.
You slide the bolt home as quietly as you can.
The sound is tiny, and his head turns toward it.
He puts his face against the door.
You step back with both hands over your mouth.
When the knocking stops, he does not go away.
You can hear him breathing on the other side.
You make one decision with your back against that door.
You are not opening it.
And you are not staying here.
From the window you can see the old water tower on the ridge.
You decide to reach it before dark.
You take the backpack from the bottom of the closet.
You fill every bottle you own at the kitchen tap.
The water runs for four seconds and then stops.
You add a torch, a lighter, and one warm coat.
You leave the photograph face down on the shelf.
You pull on boots and tie them twice.
At the front door, the peephole is dark now.
Something is pressed against the other side of the glass.
You do not touch the handle.
The bedroom window opens onto the fire escape.
You swing one leg over the sill, then the other.
The metal stairs complain under your weight.
Halfway down, you look up at your own window.
The curtain is still swinging where you let it go.
In the hallway behind the door, the knocking starts again.
The alley is narrow, wet, and full of bins.
A figure stands at the far end with its back to you.
It faces the bright street and does not move.
You take one step, then stop, then take another.
Your foot finds a glass jar on the stone.
It rolls once and rings against a drain.
The figure turns its head toward the sound.
You hold your breath and do not run.
It takes two steps toward you and then stops.
Your fingers close on an empty bottle.
You throw it hard toward the far end of the street.
It lands beyond the corner and shatters.
The figure turns and walks toward the noise.
Two more step out of a doorway and follow it.
You wait until the road is empty again.
Then you walk out slowly, the way they walk.
At the corner you see three of them in the square.
They move between parked cars without hurry.
A shop window is broken and the pavement is full of glass.
You keep to the shade under the awnings.
Your bag is heavy and the straps cut your shoulders.
One of them turns as you pass, and you keep your head down.
You match their slow, dragging step.
You do not look at their faces.
The street begins to climb toward the ridge.
The houses thin out and the road turns to dirt.
The sun is lower when you reach the last wall.
The water tower stands above you on its thin legs.
You climb the ladder without looking down.
At the top, the whole city lies under a veil of smoke.
No sirens, no engines, no voices at all.
You find your own window in the grid below.
The curtain moves behind the glass.
You left that window open.
You are not inside that room.
Something is.
Then you look at the road below the tower.
A line of figures is coming up the hill.
They follow the sound your boots made on the ladder.
You have one hour of daylight left.
And one way down.
