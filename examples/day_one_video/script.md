# Day One

> The first night, you are only supposed to stay alive.

**"Hello. It's Mr Adeyemi. Can you let me in."**

**"Hello. It's Mr Adeyemi. Can you let me in."**

**Day one.**

It is 11:48 on a Thursday. You are in bed with the sound off, three videos into something you will not remember tomorrow.

The message comes from a number you have not spoken to in two years. Two words and a link. You tap it. It does not load. You try again. It does not load.

Then the group chat goes. Forty messages in a minute, every one of them asking the same question in different words. Is this real.

Then the first video. Nine seconds, filmed from a doorway, too dark to prove anything. You watch it. You watch it again. You find a second one, the same street from a different window. You find a third, and the third is a hoax, because somebody has already made a hoax, and now you cannot tell which is which.

This is the part you will want back. You spend an hour and ten minutes on your phone, in bed, trying to find out what is happening. You refresh. You read. You type a reply and delete it. Your battery goes from seventy one to forty three. At no point do you get up.

At 12:58 the signal goes. One bar, then a thin grey nothing where the bars were, and the phone goes on being a bright rectangle with no world attached to it. You get up.

The flat is quiet in a way it has never been, because the fridge has stopped. The little green light on the router is out. The lift, when you press it, does not come.

You go to the window. The street is empty except for one car stopped at an angle with the driver's door open and its lights still on. You watch that car for a long time. Nobody comes back for it.

Down the end of the road, a woman is walking. She is not running. She is walking in a straight line, at one steady speed, past her own front door, and she does not stop when you knock on the glass.

Then, two floors down, somebody starts calling a name. It is a woman's voice, and she is out in the hall, and she is calling the same name over and over the way you call a child who has wandered off. She is not screaming. She is being very patient about it. She calls it eleven times.

You turn the music up. You do not open your door. You sit on the floor with your back against it and you turn the music up, and you tell yourself you cannot know what is out there. The calling stops.

You lock up. Chain on. Gas off at the meter. Torch out of the drawer and into your pocket. You put the phone face down on the table and you leave it there, and it does not matter, because the phone stopped being useful at 12:58.

You sit with your back to the wall until the light goes. You learn your building. The fourth floorboard. The distance to the stairwell door. The difference between a pipe and a footstep. At some point you notice you can hear your own heart, and you are embarrassed by how loud it is.

Around three in the morning you pick the phone up to check the time, and you see thirty four percent, and you put it back down. You will not charge it again. There is nothing to charge it from.

Then everything goes quiet for hours, and the quiet is worse than the noise.

At seven the sun comes up and the street looks completely normal. The car is still there. The woman is not. Twelve percent.

At eight, someone knocks on your door. Three times. Evenly spaced. The way a neighbour knocks.

You stand in the hallway with one hand flat on the wall. It is Mr Adeyemi from 4B. You know his voice. Six years of good mornings.

**"Hello. It's Mr Adeyemi. Can you let me in."**

You do not move.

**"Hello. It's Mr Adeyemi. Can you let me in."**

Same words. Same voice. The same pause in the same place.

**"Hello. It's Mr Adeyemi. Can you let me in."**

Every forty seconds. Word for word. A man you have said good morning to for six years, standing on the other side of a door, saying the same sentence on a timer.

**"Hello. It's Mr Adeyemi. Can you let me in."**

You stand in your own hallway and listen to it for an hour. Day one is over.
