You wake up because something is missing, not because something happened.
The city is not loud anymore.
There is no traffic under the window.
No lift moving in the shaft, no water in the pipes.
The room is exactly the way you left it last night.
That is the first thing that frightens you.

You sit still and listen for four minutes.
The only sound left in the world is your own breathing.
You get up slowly, because slow is the only thing you own that is still worth anything.
You put your hand on the bedroom door.
It is shut, and it is locked, and you know you did not lock it.

There is a gap under the door, and the corridor light lies in it, thin and steady.
Through the hinge you can see across the hall to the apartment opposite.
Their door is open.
So is every other door on this corridor.
Someone is standing in that doorway with their arms down and their head tilted, facing your door.
They do not knock, and they do not leave, and they do not move at all.
That is the first thing you understand about them: they do not get bored.

You sit with your back against the wall and count what you have.
Half a bottle of water, and a phone at nineteen percent.
Two crackers, and you eat one and put the other one back.
A window that opens onto a drainpipe.
A door that will hold if nobody leans on it.
There is enough here for three days if you are lucky and do nothing at all.
Lucky is not a plan.

Then the phone rings.
It is the loudest thing that has ever happened in this building.
It is somebody alive, calling you, from somewhere in the city.
You look at it.
Answering is a decision you would make with your whole body.
You do not make it.
The ringing goes on for eleven seconds and then stops.
In the corridor, the one standing in the opposite doorway turns its head toward your door.

That is how you learn the rule, in one breath, with nobody there to explain it.
Sound is a hand on the shoulder, sound moves them, and silence keeps you.
You slide the phone under the mattress and sit on it.
You can feel it buzzing against your leg like a second heartbeat.
You let it ring, and you let it ring.

By the afternoon the building is louder than the street.
There are footsteps in the stairwell, uneven, patient, taking the steps one at a time.
Nothing here runs, they move like water finding a level, and the level is this floor.
You count them by their feet and you stop counting at eleven.
Every one of them is somebody who used to live here.

In the afternoon the sun crosses the floor.
For twenty minutes you sit in the light and feel like a person.
Then it is gone, and the room is a room again.

Just before dark, someone knocks on your door.
Three slow knocks, a pause, and three more.
That is a rhythm a person would use, and a thing with nothing to say would not.
Then a voice you know says your name.
It is quiet, and careful, and it asks you to open the door.
You do not move.
Not because you have decided anything, but because your body has.

The knocking comes again, and stops, and comes again, and stops.
Outside, feet keep going past your door in both directions, and none of them stop.
You sit in the dark and understand that the door was never the problem.
The room is the problem.
This room has exactly one exit.
The exit is the corridor.
The corridor is full of people who are now nothing but a direction.
Outside your window there is a drainpipe.
Three floors of wet metal, and a street that is not empty.
The room buys you days, and the pipe buys you a morning.
That is the whole trade, and nobody is going to make it for you.

You spend the last of the light getting ready.
You put water in your pockets and pull socks over your hands.
You open the window as far as it will go without a sound.
Then you wait.
You wait until the second hour of dark, when the feet have thinned out.
You wait until the building is quiet the way a held breath is quiet.
Then you put one leg out into the cold.
Behind you the room is warm, and locked, and holds everything you know.
You do not look back at it.
The first day ends with you on a wall.
Three floors above a street full of people standing still.
