# THE LAST BUS OF THE NIGHT

*Second person, present tense. Played completely straight. Nobody has a name.*

---

The depot clock says 2:14 and the last bus of the night is yours.

Route 12. Nine miles of ring road, twenty-two stops, and nobody on board but you.

You have a flask, a route card, and a clipboard you have never once needed.

The radio makes a sound like a held breath.

**Route 12.**

The first stop is the retail park. A man is standing at the kerb with his hands at his sides.

He has no coat. It is four degrees.

You open the doors. He gets on, and he does not look at you, and he does not pay, and he sits at the back.

You say: fare, please. He says nothing.

You have driven this route for nine years. You have heard every excuse there is.

You let it go. At two in the morning you let everything go.

At the second stop there are three of them.

They get on one after another, evenly spaced, like they queued.

Not one of them pays. Not one of them looks at you.

They sit in the back row, facing forward, in the four seats behind the last man.

You check the mirror. Four people, and every one of them sat down in the seat behind the one before.

You pick up the radio. You say: control, this is twelve.

Nothing. You try again.

A voice comes back that is not control, and it is not a person, and it says: do not stop.

The radio clicks off.

You look at the mirror. Four people. Not one of them has moved.

Stop three is the hospital entrance. There is nobody on the pavement.

You slow down anyway, out of nine years of habit.

The bell rings.

The bell rings twice, from the back of an empty bus, and the four of them are standing up.

You do not stop. You put your foot down.

At stop four they are standing in the road.

Eleven of them, shoulder to shoulder, waiting for the doors.

You drive through them, and you do not feel anything, and that is the part you will think about later.

The interior light goes out.

You drive the next mile in the dark with the mirror showing nothing at all.

Then the light comes back on, and the bus is full.

Every seat. Every handrail. Standing in the aisle all the way to the back window.

And the bell is ringing the whole time, at the front, where the button is.

Then a woman's voice, from the back: driver.

Real. Tired. Ordinary.

She says: I need to get off. I've got a kid at home and I need to get off.

Her voice is the only thing on that bus that sounds like it came out of a person.

You stop in the middle of the ring road. You open the doors.

Nobody moves.

You say: this is your stop.

And she says the thing you are going to hear for the rest of your life.

It's not my stop. It's yours.

You look at the route card. Twenty-two stops. You have done sixteen.

You have driven this exact route, this exact shift, four nights a week, for nine years.

And the bus is pointing the wrong way.

The last stop on the card is where you live. Two streets from your own front door.

Twenty past three in the morning, with a full bus, at your own stop.

You have never once looked at that stop. You have driven past it a thousand times with the doors shut, because you finish at the depot.

So there is a decision, and there is only one of it.

You can stop at your own street, open the doors, and find out what gets off.

Or you can drive past it and take the whole thing to the depot and let the depot deal with it.

You drive past your own street.

You do not slow down. You do not look in the mirror.

Nobody rings the bell.

That is worse than the ringing.

The depot lights come up ahead and the sky behind them is going grey.

You pull in. You stop. You sit there with both hands on the wheel.

You look in the mirror.

The bus is empty.

Every seat. Every handrail. Nothing.

There is one wet footprint on the floor halfway down the aisle, and then nothing else.

The depot is empty. The office light is on.

Your coat is already on the hook by the door.

Your keys are already in the board, in your slot, with your number on the tag.

Your shift ended at half two.

Somebody finished your shift for you.

You are standing in the yard with your flask in one hand and nothing at all in the other.

And the bus starts itself.

The lights come on inside it, one after another, all the way down.

The doors open.

You are the last driver in the depot, and it is waiting.
