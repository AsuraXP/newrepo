# DAY ONE

*Second person, present tense. Grounded. Nothing supernatural.*

---

The fridge stops at 12:58. You know the time because you were looking at your phone when the kitchen light went out. Then the router. Then the small red dot on the front of the telly that you had never once noticed until it was gone. And then the flat is so quiet that you can hear your own blood.

**Day one.**

Seventy minutes earlier you were in bed with the sound off. A message came in from a number you had not spoken to in two years. You had deleted the contact. The number still knew where you were. Two words and a link. Come outside. The link did not load. You pressed it again, and watched it not load again.

Then the group chat went. Forty messages in a minute, all asking the same thing. Then the videos started. The first was nine seconds long and too dark to prove anything. The second was clearer. A man outside a shop, standing up. And then on the pavement, and nobody going to help him. The third was a hoax. Somebody had already made a hoax, and now you could not tell which was which. You put the phone down on the bed, and picked it straight back up.

You watched for an hour and ten minutes and you did not get up once. At 12:58 the signal went. One bar, then nothing. Your phone stayed bright and became useless in your hand. The lift did not come. Nobody in the building used the lift at one in the morning, and you still noticed. You put your hand flat on the wall. It was still warm from the day. At one twenty the entryphone buzzed. You lifted it and said nothing into it, and it buzzed again, and then it stopped.

You went to the window, because everybody goes to the window. The street was empty apart from one car stopped at an angle, driver's door open, headlights still on. Nobody came back for it. Down the end of the road, a woman was walking. She walked past her own front door and did not stop. Her left arm hung wrong. It swung a beat behind the rest of her.

You rang the police. It rang twice, and then a recorded voice told you the line was busy. You rang your mother. It rang until it stopped. The landline gave you a dial tone that went nowhere.

**Then, two floors down, somebody started calling a name. A woman's voice, out in the hall, patient, the way you call a child who has wandered off. She called it eleven times. You counted, because there was nothing else to do with your hands. On the twelfth she stopped in the middle of the word. You waited for the rest of it. The rest of it did not come.**

You put the chain on. You put the back of your head against the door and listened to the building. Somewhere above you a tap was running. Somewhere below, a dog. At three a car alarm went off down the road. It ran for twenty minutes, and then it stopped, and the quiet after it was worse. That was all. That was the whole night.

The sun came back at six, the way it does, without asking. You turned on the tap. Water came out, cold and ordinary. You turned it off and did not think about it again. You found the torch, the batteries, and the little radio that takes six of them. A helicopter went over, very high and very far away. It did not come back.

Then you did the maths, because the maths was the only thing left that could be done. Four tins of soup. Half a bag of rice. The end of a loaf. Two litres of Coke and not one bottle of water. Three days if you were careful. Four if you were lying to yourself.

There is a shop on the corner. It is not open. There is a place ten minutes down the road with a pharmacy in it, and a food hall, and a car park with lights, and a roof you could see from the next street. You have been in it a hundred times. You have never once thought about it. It has everything you need, and you know the way there with your eyes shut. Ten minutes. You could be there before the streets are properly awake.

Or you could stay. The door is solid. The chain holds. Nothing out there gets through a door that is locked. You sat with that for an hour. You made a list. You crossed the list out. At half five a man walked his dog past the end of your road. Neither of them looked up.

First light. Tomorrow, at six, before the streets fill. You got the bag out from under the bed and put it by the door. Torch. Batteries. The radio. The bread knife out of the drawer, because there was nothing better. The walk was ten minutes. Getting to the walk was four days.

You lay on top of the covers with your shoes on and listened to the building do nothing. At some point the tap stopped dripping. It had been dripping for two years.

At eight the next morning, before you had reached for the bag, somebody knocked. Three times. Evenly spaced. The way a neighbour knocks. It was Mr Adeyemi from 4B. You knew his voice. Hello. It's Mr Adeyemi. Can you let me in. You did not answer.
