# NINE FLOORS

*Episode one. Second person, present tense. Played completely straight.*
*No names. No explanation. One day, one problem, one decision.*

---

You are the caretaker of a nine-storey block, and you live in the basement.

You are also the man who says morning to everybody, which is not in the job description, and which nobody has ever asked you to stop doing.

Nine floors. Fifty-four flats. One lift that has been broken since March.

This morning the lift is still broken, the bins are out, and the street is quieter than a Tuesday should be.

A man is standing in the road at the corner, facing away, not crossing.

You say morning to him.

He does not turn round. He does not move at all. He is standing at an angle you would not stand at.

You decide he is waiting for a lift that is not coming.

You go back inside. You smile at the door as it shuts, because you always do.

**Seven twenty.**

The water stops. Not slows. Stops, mid-flow, while you are holding a cup under it.

You go down to the plant room in the basement and look at the gauge. The tank is full. The pump is fine. The mains is not.

You stand there and you do the arithmetic out loud, because you are the sort of man who does arithmetic out loud.

No mains means no water above the second floor. And the second floor is where she lives.

She is eighty-eight. She has been in that flat since before you had this job. She has a kettle she fills the night before, and this morning she will have forgotten, and by tonight there will be nothing in it.

You go up to two.

She opens the door before you knock, because she has been listening for the stairs. She asks if you have any water. You give her the bottle out of your bag, the one you brought for yourself, and you tell her you will sort it.

She says: don't go down there.

You say: it's the plant room, it's my job.

She says: don't go down there on your own.

**Seven fifty.**

There is a valve in the plant room, on the floor, behind the tank. It has to be turned by hand, forty turns, and it has not been turned since the last time the mains went, which was 2019, and which you did on your own then, and which took you four minutes.

Down is nine floors. Up is nine floors. There is no other way to get water into this building today.

Or you can walk out of the front door, get in the van, and drive to the shop on the corner and buy every bottle on the shelf.

The shop is a two-minute drive. It is also the way the man at the corner is facing.

You stand in the hall and you look at the front door and you look at the stairs.

You go down.

The stairwell lights are on a timer and the timer is on the mains, so the stairwell is dark, and you have the torch off your belt, and that is the whole of your equipment.

Six flights. You count them out loud, because you are the sort of man who counts out loud.

On the landing between five and four, somebody is standing in the corner.

Facing the wall. Facing into the corner like a child being punished.

You say: you alright, mate?

You say it before you have thought about it, because you are the man who says morning to everybody.

He turns around. His head gets there first and his body takes a moment to agree with it.

And you understand, all at once and far too late, that you have been walking down a stairwell making a sound every nine steps.

You go back up.

You are faster than he is. You are faster than all of them, and there are more than one of them now, and that is the only advantage you have and it will not last.

You get to your own floor and put your hand on the door and the door does not move, because your keys are on the table inside, because you never lock it, because you have never once needed to.

You go up instead.

Six. Seven. Eight.

You come out on the roof, and the roof is flat and grey and empty, and you pull the door shut behind you and sit down against it.

And you wait, and you listen, and after a while the sound of the stairs goes quiet.

**Nine forty.**

You go back down.

All nine floors, in the dark, one hand on the wall, counting your own steps, and you do not breathe properly until your feet are on the basement concrete.

The plant room door is where you left it. The valve is where you left it.

Forty turns. You do them with both hands and your teeth shut.

The pump wakes up.

You stand in the dark and listen to the pipes fill, all the way up the building, floor by floor, like somebody walking up the stairs.

Then you hear it.

Two floors above you, a tap is running.

You know every tap in this building. You know the one in two that runs a bit rough, and the one on seven that somebody leaves dripping.

This one is on two.

You stand there and you listen to it run, and you start to smile, because she has heard the water come back on and she has filled her kettle, and that is the whole of what you wanted today.

Then the tap stops.

Nobody turns a tap off that fast.

You are standing in the basement of a nine-storey building with a torch in one hand, and every pipe in the wall above you has gone quiet, and she is eighty-eight, and she is on two, and she has been listening for the stairs.

You go up.
