# TWENTY-FOUR HOUR ACCESS

*Second person, present tense. Played completely straight. No fantasy. Nobody has a name.*

---

It is twenty to midnight and you are moving your life into a metal box.

Unit two-one-seven. Ten feet by ten. Everything that mattered last year.

Twenty-four hour access, the sign says, and it is the only place in the city still open.

You have a trolley, a padlock, and a phone with eleven percent.

The man on the desk gives you the gate code and tells you the lights run on sensors.

You say: sensors?

He says: they come on when you move. So keep moving.

He is not being funny.

**Twenty-four hour access.**

The gate comes down behind you. Roller steel, one and a half seconds.

The corridor is ten feet wide and forty units long and every shutter is the same shutter.

Twenty-two on the left. Twenty-two on the right.

You start loading. Boxes, a chair, a bike. The trolley squeaks.

You are halfway through when the lights go out.

Then they come back on, because you moved.

You stand in the middle of the corridor holding a box, and you notice your phone has no signal down here.

Not weak. None.

So you carry on, because the alternative is standing still.

At eleven fifty you hear the gate go up.

Then footsteps, fast, and then not fast.

Then the banging.

It starts at unit four and walks down the row, shutter after shutter, like someone going along a fence with a stick.

You stop. The lights go out.

You move. They come back on.

At the far end, forty feet away, someone is standing with their back to you, facing unit two-two-three.

Their left arm hangs lower than their right.

Their head turns first, and then the rest of them.

You are standing in the middle of a corridor with a trolley.

You do not run. You push the trolley into unit two-one-seven and pull the shutter down.

That shutter is the loudest thing you have ever done.

You sit in the dark with your back against it and you listen.

And then, from the unit beside yours, so close it could be inside your own head:

Knocking. Three times. Evenly spaced.

Then a voice. A man's voice. Tired, ordinary, muffled by steel.

He says: I can hear you breathing.

He says: I've been in here since seven.

He says: open it. My latch is on the outside.

You know that already. Every latch on this row is on the outside.

You put your palm flat on the wall between the two units.

And the knocking changes.

It is coming from both sides now. It is coming from up the row and down the row, from units that were empty when you walked past them ten minutes ago.

The lights are off and the voice is still asking.

He says: are you there.

He says it nine times.

On the ninth you take your hand off the wall.

You have a shutter you can close and a gate code you can remember.

You can sit here until six and let the morning sort it out.

Or you can go now, in the dark, the way they came in, while they are all busy knocking.

Forty feet. Both hands on the wall. Count the shutters.

You go.

Six. Seven. Eight. Somebody is breathing on the other side of number nine.

You do not stop for nine.

Fifteen. Sixteen. The corridor smells like wet coats.

Twenty-one. Twenty-two. You can feel the draught from the vehicle gate on the back of your neck.

The keypad lights up when you touch it.

Four numbers. You have them. You watched him write them down.

It does not take.

You press them again, slower.

It does not take.

The padlock on the gate has a sticker on it that you did not read on the way in.

It says the code changes at midnight.

Twenty-four hour access.

It is twelve oh nine.

The lights come back on, all forty feet of them at once, because you moved.

And the corridor behind you is full.

The voice beside unit two-one-eight says the last thing you will hear before you leave this place.

It says: I told you. Open it.

You are forty feet from a shutter with a latch on the outside, and the keypad is still glowing, and it is twelve oh nine.
